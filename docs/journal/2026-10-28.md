## "Should we stop?"

No. A failed **registered hypothesis** stops; the programme does not. SUTRA's
particular implementation — 64 top-1 rules, a 1,024-wide general path, 763
steps — is a null. The idea of a learned rule grammar is not disproved; the
evidence says this version spent capacity without producing specialized rules.

The next attempt must change one thing and be pre-registered:
- rule specialization/diversity (the uniform router is the failure signal);
- a general path matched to the control's 1,408 width;
- a longer 1,500-step micro;
- or role-conditioned routing.

The X36 memory has the same shape: retention passes, language cost fails. The
next version needs a val-preservation mechanism, not a re-tune.

## The looping question — the cheap test already ran

The user asked whether more research could produce a next-gen LLM. The
research scan found four real candidates, but the most useful finding was in
our own record: on 2026-09-23 the architecture ablation already ran a
**6-layer × 2-loop** arm at 29,921,280 params. It scored **3.5012** against the
control's **3.4924** at step 700, **+0.009**, one seed. The pre-registered
promotion bar was ≤ control − 0.02, so looping was correctly called
quality-neutral and did not enter v0.2.0. The cost of the second pass bought
nothing at that scale.

That is not the end of X11. D24's design was "5 unique layers × 2 loops vs 10
unique layers at equal effective depth; then 10 × 2 vs 10 × 1." The simple
6 × 2 vs 6 × 1 test asks whether extra compute at equal parameters helps; it
does not ask whether looping is **parameter-efficient**. The missing test is
**6 × 2 at 30M vs 12 × 1 at ~60M**: equal effective depth, half the
parameters. If the looped model matches the 60M dense, looping buys a 2×
parameter reduction even though it is slightly worse than the 30M dense. That
is the Ouro claim at our scale, and it has never been run.

## What the literature says about scale

| mechanism | reported result | smallest published scale | fit to us |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Looped / recurrent depth** (Ouro/LoopLM; Geiping et al.) | 1.4B/2.6B match up to 12B standard; gains in composition/manipulation; depth extrapolation at inference | 1.4B (recurrent-depth 3.5B) | code exists; cheap; the missing test is parameter efficiency |
| **Mixture-of-Recursions** (Bae et al., 2025) | shared layers + per-token recursion; Pareto frontier at equal FLOPs | 135M-1.7B | boldest; directly tests SUTRA's uniform-router failure |
| **Memory Layers at Scale** (Berges et al., 2024/25) | sparse PKM beats dense with >2× compute; factual gains | 134M, up to 1T tokens | fixes X36's val cost, but starved at 500M tokens; unstable for small bases |
| **Hyper-connections** (Zhu et al., ICLR 2025) | learned residual streams; ~half the tokens for comparable quality | 1B/7B (100M in a 2026 follow-up) | cheap; sample-efficiency; a component, not a new generation |

The uncomfortable part: all of these are validated at **134M-1.7B**, not
30M. Our micro is 30M params and ~50M tokens, about **1.7 tokens/param** and
roughly 8% of Chinchilla-optimal. At that regime capacity mechanisms are
starved and routers do not get enough signal. The cheap looping test was
neutral; the parameter-efficiency test may also be neutral at 30M. That is
why the next test must include the 60M dense control and, if it passes, scale
to 58M.

## Compute reality

A 1B Chinchilla-style run is ~1.2e20 FLOPs; at ~23 TFLOPS effective and
30 GPU-h/week, that is **tens of weeks** for one run. A 7B/140B-token model is
~6e21 FLOPs, **decades** at this quota. A frontier model is millions of times
beyond that. Research narrows the search; it does not close the compute gap.
The realistic output of this programme is a validated mechanism that someone
with scale can use, not a frontier model this month.

## Invention portfolio — a mechanism, not a frontier model

The user clarified the goal: not a frontier model, but a novel mechanism that
makes the 58M model interesting enough for a paper. The portfolio is in
`docs/research/2026-10-28-invention-portfolio.md`.

The first bet is the **Counterfactual Residual Bottleneck (CRB)**: split the
residual stream into a low-rank stable subspace and a volatile surface
subspace, then train the stable one to be invariant to meaning-preserving
edits (consistent renames, synonyms) and sensitive to meaning-changing ones
(subject/object swap, negation). It targets X36's val cost and SUTRA's uniform
router without a separate memory or a discrete router. The repo already has the
probes: swap sensitivity, entity retention, CL-0.

Stage 1 is a 30M four-arm screen (control, split-only, loss-only, CRB) at two
seeds, ~8 GPU-h. Stage 2 is a 58M control-vs-CRB confirmation at two seeds,
~16-20 GPU-h. The bars are C-1 val, C-2 swap sensitivity, C-3 paraphrase
invariance, C-4 retention, C-5 stable-subspace rank. If C-1/C-2 pass at 58M,
the paper claim is a workshop-level result: a counterfactual bottleneck
improves systematic sensitivity without a language-modelling tax. If it fails,
the next candidates are Relational Algebra Attention and Predictive-Coding
Depth; the portfolio exists to stop us tuning the last idea.
