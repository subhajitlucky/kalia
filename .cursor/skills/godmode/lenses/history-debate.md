# History and debate lens — sources, arguments, narratives

Use for historical claims, essays, policy debates, and any argument where
interpretation matters.

## Sources

- What is the primary source? Who wrote it, when, for whom, and why?
- What is the provenance? Is it complete, edited, translated, or excerpted?
- What is the secondary source’s bias and method?
- What is the difference between a fact, an inference, and an interpretation?
- Date every claim. A quote without a date is a rumour with good lighting.

## Context

- What was the world like at the time: institutions, technology, incentives,
  language, and constraints?
- What did the actors know, and when did they know it?
- What is the counterfactual? Would the outcome have differed without the
  supposed cause?
- What is the scale: one case, a trend, or a population?

## Argument

- Define the terms before debating them. Most disagreements are definitional.
- Steelman the strongest opposing case; then answer it.
- Separate the claim from the person making it.
- Identify the load-bearing premise: if it falls, does the argument fall?
- Check for false dichotomy, straw man, circularity, survivorship, and
  post-hoc reasoning.
- What evidence would change your mind? If nothing, it is ideology, not
  analysis.

## Debate

- State the proposition in one sentence both sides accept.
- Agree on the facts and the criteria before arguing about conclusions.
- Attack the argument, not the motive.
- Concede what is true; it makes the remaining disagreement sharper.
- Distinguish “I don’t know” from “it is unknowable.”

## Output

Answer first. Then the strongest evidence for, the strongest evidence against,
the unresolved uncertainty, and the judgment. Do not pretend the evidence is
cleaner than it is.
