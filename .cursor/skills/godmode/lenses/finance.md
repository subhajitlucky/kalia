# Finance lens — markets, money, risk

Use for trades, budgets, valuations, business models, pricing, and risk
decisions.

## Frame

- What is the goal: return, income, liquidity, hedge, learning, optionality?
- What is the time horizon and the currency?
- What is the maximum acceptable loss? Is the downside capped?
- Who is the counterparty, and what are their incentives?

## The basics

- **Base rate first.** What usually happens to this kind of asset, business, or
  trade? A story without a base rate is marketing.
- **Expected value is not enough.** Consider the distribution: skew, tail,
  drawdown, and path dependency.
- **Compounding and fees.** Small annual fees and taxes dominate long horizons.
- **Liquidity.** Can you exit at the price you see? What is the slippage?
- **Leverage.** It magnifies the path, not only the return. What is the
  margin-call or ruin point?
- **Incentives.** Who is paid to sell this? What is the conflict of interest?

## Scenarios

Build at least three: base, upside, downside. For each:

- probability (rough, calibrated, honest);
- payoff;
- what would have to be true;
- the leading indicator that would tell you early.

Add the “ruin” scenario: what makes the decision irreversible? Avoid it unless
the upside is exceptional and the downside is truly capped.

## Decision

- Position size so that being wrong is survivable.
- Prefer asymmetric, reversible, liquid exposures.
- State the kill criteria before entering: price, thesis, time, or evidence.
- Separate the quality of the asset from the price you pay.
- Do not confuse a good story with a good expected value.

## Biases to check

- Recency, anchoring, confirmation, survivorship, narrative fallacy, loss
  aversion, overconfidence, sunk cost, and the temptation to average down.
- “Everyone is doing it” is a base-rate question, not an argument.

## Output

Answer first. Then the thesis, the base rate, the scenarios, the downside, the
position/decision, and the kill criteria. Name the evidence that would change
your mind.
